How the map was made

It maps buildings, not believers, and it is only as complete as OpenStreetMap.

Where the sites come from

Every point comes from OpenStreetMap, the volunteer-built world map. On the 1st of each month a script asks the Overpass API for every feature tagged amenity=place_of_worship inside the New York City boundary (OpenStreetMap relation 175905):

[out:json][timeout:180];
area(3600175905)->.nyc;
(
  node["amenity"="place_of_worship"](area.nyc);
  way["amenity"="place_of_worship"](area.nyc);
  relation["amenity"="place_of_worship"](area.nyc);
);
out center tags;

The latest pull returned … features. Some congregations are mapped twice, as a point and as a building outline, or as a church and its parish hall under the same name. Where two features share a name and either the same city building or sit within 80 meters of each other, the map keeps one, preferring the copy with a photo or more detail. That removed …, leaving … sites.

How sites are grouped

OpenStreetMap tags each site with a religion (christian, jewish, muslim and so on) and, often, a denomination. The processing script, process.py, maps those to eight traditions and a shorter list of denominations.

  • Catholic combines catholic, roman_catholic and greek_catholic.
  • Orthodox Christian combines the Greek, Russian, Romanian, Serbian, Ukrainian, Albanian, Bulgarian, Antiochian, Coptic, Ethiopian, Syriac and Armenian churches.
  • Methodist includes United Methodist, African Methodist Episcopal, AME Zion and Free Methodist. Pentecostal includes Assemblies of God and the Church of God.
  • Congregational/UCC combines congregational and United Church of Christ. Smaller named denominations (Church of Christ, Nazarene, Apostolic and others) go to Other Christian rather than to Christian, unspecified, because they do name a denomination.
  • A handful of tagging slips are corrected: a site tagged with a Catholic religion value, a mosque tagged mue, and sites that carry a denomination such as Baptist or Jewish but no religion.

The gaps are large. … Christian sites, … of them, carry no denomination and appear as Christian, unspecified. That reflects missing tags, not nondenominational congregations. Only … of the … Muslim sites are tagged Sunni, Shia or another tradition, and most Jewish sites carry no movement.

Photos

… sites have a photo. Each comes from Wikimedia Commons: where OpenStreetMap links a site to its Wikidata entry, the map uses that entry's main image. Every photo is credited to its photographer with its license and links to its Commons page. Most show the facade; a few show an interior or a wider view, because that is what the Wikidata entry uses.

Sites without a photo link to Google Street View at the site's location, which opens the nearest street-level panorama. It may need turning to face the building.

Building dates and architects

Each site is matched to a building in the city's building footprints file, by the city building number OpenStreetMap carries or, failing that, by which footprint the point falls inside. … sites matched. The file gives a construction year, but many of those years are estimates: citywide, about 112,000 buildings are listed as 1930 and about 37,000 each as 1931 and 1935, against a few thousand for neighboring years, and round decades are often guesses (St. Patrick's Cathedral, finished in 1878, is listed as 1910). The map shows a year only when it is a specific one, which leaves … sites with a construction year. Even those can be wrong, or can date a later replacement building.

For sites linked to Wikidata, the popup also gives Wikidata's start date and architect. That date is sometimes the founding of the congregation rather than the building: Trinity Church shows 1698.

What this map is not

  • Not a map of believers. A neighborhood with many storefront churches is not necessarily more Christian than one with a single large parish.
  • Not weighted by size. A cathedral and a one-room storefront church are the same dot.
  • Not complete. Many Hasidic shtiebels meet in unmarked rooms and apartments, and many mosques rent commercial space; both are often missing from OpenStreetMap. Some closed sites are still mapped.

Fixing errors

Every popup links to the site's OpenStreetMap entry. Anyone with a free account can correct a name, add a denomination or mark a site closed, and the map picks up the change at the next monthly refresh.

Rebuilding it

python3 fetch_raw.py   # OpenStreetMap -> raw.json
python3 enrich.py      # Wikidata, Commons, building footprints -> enrich.json
python3 process.py     # classify, de-duplicate, merge -> data.json

All three sources are free and need no key. The code is on GitHub.

Better sources for particular traditions

  • Christian congregations and adherents by county: the U.S. Religion Census (2020).
  • Catholic parishes: the Archdiocese of New York and the Diocese of Brooklyn.
  • Jewish New York: the UJA-Federation Jewish Community Study of New York (2023).
  • Religious buildings in property records: building class M in the city's PLUTO file.
